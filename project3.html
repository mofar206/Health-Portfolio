DESIGN QUESTIONS TO TAKE FORWARD

Access is more than
a point on a map.

01 / MOBILITY

Measure the actual trip.

Walking, transit, and driving times can tell different stories. Account for vehicle availability and physical access.

02 / FOOD CHOICE

Look beyond store counts.

Consider affordability, opening hours, culturally relevant options, and benefit acceptance.

03 / EVIDENCE

Test the relationship.

Align data years and geographic units, quantify uncertainty, and examine confounding factors before drawing conclusions.

METHOD & SOURCES

Make the reasoning
as visible as the result.

A clear distinction between what the prototype demonstrates and what the data can support.

01

A shared comparison

Both neighborhood selectors use the same six-record scenario dataset, so every displayed difference is computed consistently.

02

Explicit units

Distances use miles; health and poverty differences use percentage points. Comparing a neighborhood with itself returns zero differences.

03

No automatic designation

ZIP-level scenarios are not official USDA low-income/low-access classifications. Source documentation is linked for the appropriate geography and definitions.

Data provenance & limitations +

The original neighborhood figures do not include extraction records, uncertainty estimates, or a reproducible join to USDA and CDC source data. This prototype makes no official food-access designation and does not imply that grocery distance causes diabetes or obesity. A research-ready analysis would align tract boundaries, source vintages, denominator populations, and relevant covariates.

  • Original portfolio dataset ↗

    Neighborhood scenarios are hard-coded in the original project. No row-level citations or extraction records accompany them.

  • U.S. Census Bureau · ACS 2022 ↗

  • CDC PLACES ↗

  • USDA · Food Access Research Atlas documentation ↗

    The Atlas documents geography, access measures, source years, and limitations. Consult it before classifying a place.
